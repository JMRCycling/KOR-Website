# Bike Tune-Up Cost: What Shops Charge & What's Included

A bike tune-up runs $50 to $100 for a basic adjustment, $100 to $180 for a standard tune-up, and $200 or more for a full overhaul — and that's before parts. Which tier you need depends on how long it's been since the last service and what's actually worn versus just out of adjustment. Here's what each tier covers, what parts cost on top, and how to avoid paying overhaul prices for a problem a basic tune-up would have caught.

## The Three Tune-Up Tiers

### Basic Tune-Up ($50–$100)

A basic tune-up covers adjustments, not replacements: derailleur and brake indexing, gear cable tension, brake pad alignment, tire pressure, and a safety check of bolts and quick releases. Most shops also true wheels lightly and lubricate the chain as part of this tier. It's the right call for a bike that shifts a little rough or squeaks but has no worn components — think of it as a reset, not a repair.

### Standard Tune-Up ($100–$180)

Standard tune-ups add a deeper clean and inspection on top of everything in the basic tier: degreasing and relubricating the drivetrain, a full wheel true, headset and bottom bracket adjustment, and brake pad inspection with replacement if needed. Shops typically disassemble more of the bike for this tier, which is why it costs more in labor even before any parts are swapped. This is the right tier for a bike that hasn't been serviced in 6–12 months or is showing multiple small issues at once.

### Overhaul ($200+)

A full overhaul strips the bike down further — pulling the drivetrain apart for cleaning, repacking or replacing bearings in the hubs, headset, and bottom bracket, and inspecting cables and housing for replacement. Shops usually quote this tier per-bike after a look-over, since the labor time depends heavily on how much needs to come apart. Overhauls make sense for a bike that's a year or more overdue for service, one you just bought used (see [how to buy a used bike](/articles/how-to-buy-a-used-bike) for what to check before you even get it home), or one being brought back into service after storage.

## Parts Cost Extra

Every tier above covers labor and adjustment — not replacement parts. If your chain, cassette, brake pads, or cables are actually worn out rather than just dirty or misadjusted, those get quoted and charged separately. A worn chain and cassette can add $60–$150 to a tune-up bill; brake pads run $15–$40 a pair including installation. See [how long do bike parts last](/articles/how-long-do-bike-parts-last) for realistic mileage ranges on the components most likely to show up as an add-on charge, so a shop quote doesn't catch you off guard.

## Which Tier Do You Actually Need?

Match the tier to your last service date and symptoms, not to what sounds thorough:

- **It's been under 6 months, and shifting or braking feels slightly off** — basic tune-up.
- **It's been 6–12 months, or you're noticing multiple small issues (noise, drag, minor play)** — standard tune-up.
- **It's been over a year, you bought the bike used, or it's coming out of long-term storage** — overhaul.

Following a [maintenance schedule](/articles/bike-maintenance-schedule) keyed to mileage rather than waiting for something to feel wrong is what keeps most riders in the cheaper tiers — problems caught early are adjustments; problems caught late are replacements.

## Preventive Scheduling Beats Emergency Repairs

A scheduled tune-up booked in advance is almost always cheaper and faster than a walk-in repair after something fails mid-ride. Shops charge a premium for same-day or emergency work, and a chain that snaps on the trail can turn into cassette and derailleur damage that a basic swap would have prevented entirely. Riders who track wear and book service ahead of a failure typically pay for adjustments; riders who wait for something to break pay for adjustments plus the failure's collateral damage, on a rushed timeline. If you don't want to track mileage and wear yourself, a [personal maintenance plan](/personal-plans) does the reminders for you, so tune-ups happen on a schedule instead of in response to a problem.

## FAQ

**How often should I get a bike tune-up?**
Most riders do well with a basic tune-up every 3–6 months and a standard tune-up or overhaul once a year, adjusted for mileage — a bike ridden daily needs service more often than one ridden on weekends.

**Is a tune-up worth it on an older bike?**
Usually yes, as long as the frame is sound. A tune-up is far cheaper than replacing a bike, and a well-maintained older bike with fresh consumables rides close to as well as a new one.

**Can I do a tune-up myself?**
Basic adjustments (derailleur indexing, brake alignment, chain lube) are approachable with a bit of practice. Bearing work and hydraulic brake bleeds are better left to a shop unless you already have the tools and experience.

**Will the shop call before charging for extra parts?**
Reputable shops quote parts separately and get approval before replacing anything beyond the agreed tune-up. Ask for this upfront if a shop doesn't offer it automatically.

**Does a tune-up include truing my wheels?**
A light true is usually included in basic and standard tiers; a wheel that's badly out of true or has broken spokes is typically quoted separately, since it can take significantly longer.
