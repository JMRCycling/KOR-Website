# Bike Maintenance for Beginners: The 8 Skills That Matter

You don't need to become a bike mechanic to keep your bike running well. Eight basic habits — done at the right cadence — catch almost everything that causes breakdowns or expensive repairs. The rest can wait for a shop visit.

This guide covers those eight skills, a starter tool kit that costs less than $100, how often to do each task, and which jobs are worth leaving to a professional even after you get comfortable with the basics.

## The 80/20 of Home Bike Maintenance

A small number of habits prevent most of the problems that send a bike to the shop. Here they are, in the order you'll use them.

### 1. Wash Your Bike

Dirt, mud, and road grit act like sandpaper on your drivetrain and pivots. A rinse with a hose (avoid pointing it directly at bearings and the bottom bracket), a soft brush, and mild soap removes the grit before it works its way into moving parts. Dry the bike with a rag afterward, especially the chain, before you re-lube it.

### 2. Lube the Chain

A dry or dirty chain wears out fast and shifts poorly. Wipe the chain down, apply a drop of chain lube to each roller, let it soak in for a few minutes, then wipe off the excess with a clean rag. Excess lube attracts more dirt than it prevents wear — the goal is a light film, not a dripping chain.

### 3. Check Tire Pressure

Underinflated tires roll slower, wear unevenly, and pinch-flat more easily; overinflated tires ride harsh and lose grip. Check pressure with a gauge before every ride — tires lose a few PSI a week even with no leak. Your tire's sidewall lists a pressure range; where you land in that range depends on your weight, tire width, and terrain.

### 4. Run the M-Check Before You Ride

The M-check is a quick front-to-back inspection: front axle secure, headset and bars tight, brakes engage before the lever hits the bar, saddle and post clamped, drivetrain shifts cleanly, rear axle secure. It takes under two minutes and catches loose bolts and brake issues before they become a problem mid-ride. The full walkthrough is in the [pre-ride bike check guide](/articles/pre-ride-bike-check).

### 5. Check Chain Wear

A stretched chain accelerates wear on your cassette and chainrings — parts that cost far more to replace than the chain itself. A chain wear indicator tool tells you in seconds whether you're still in the safe range. See [how to measure chain wear](/articles/how-to-measure-chain-wear) for the exact steps and replacement thresholds.

### 6. Glance at Your Brake Pads

Pull off the wheel (or just look through the caliper on disc brakes) and check pad thickness. Thin pads — down to the wear line or backing plate — need replacing before your next few rides, not after they start grinding on metal.

### 7. Check Bolts

Vibration works fasteners loose over time, especially on rough roads or trails. A monthly check of stem bolts, seatpost clamp, bottle cage bolts, and rack or fender mounts (if you run them) catches this before something rattles loose mid-ride.

### 8. Know When to Go to a Shop

Recognizing what's outside your skill level is itself a maintenance skill. If something feels wrong and you can't identify why after the checks above, that's the signal to get it looked at rather than keep riding on a guess.

## A Starter Tool Kit Under $100

You don't need a full home shop to cover the basics above. This gets you there:

- **Chain lube** (~$10) — wet lube for rain and mud, dry lube for dusty conditions
- **Degreaser and rags** (~$10) — for cleaning the drivetrain before relubing
- **Floor pump with a pressure gauge** (~$30–40) — far more accurate than a hand pump, and you'll use it constantly
- **Multi-tool with hex keys** (~$15–20) — covers most bolts on the bike
- **Chain wear indicator tool** (~$10–15) — the single best early-warning tool you can own
- **Tire levers and a spare tube or patch kit** (~$10) — for roadside repairs

That's a complete starter kit for well under $100, and it covers everything in the eight skills above.

## How Often to Do Each Task

Cadence matters as much as the tasks themselves. A rough schedule:

**After every wet or muddy ride:** wash and re-lube the chain. Wet conditions wash lube off and pull grit into the drivetrain fast — this is the single highest-payoff habit for riders who ride in rain or mud regularly.

**Weekly (or before every ride if you ride often):** check tire pressure and run the M-check.

**Monthly:** check chain wear, glance at brake pads, and check bolts.

A full mileage-based schedule for every component — not just the beginner basics — is in the [bike maintenance schedule](/articles/bike-maintenance-schedule), worth bookmarking once these habits feel routine.

## What Not to DIY at First

A few jobs are worth leaving to a shop until you've built more experience, tools, or both:

- **Brake bleeds** — hydraulic brake systems require the correct fluid (mineral oil or DOT, depending on brand) and a clean process; air in the line or contaminated fluid makes brakes unpredictable.
- **Suspension internals** — fork and shock service beyond a lower-leg wipe-down requires specific tools, seals, and correct oil weights. Get this wrong and you can damage an expensive component.
- **Press-fit bottom bracket work** — removing and installing press-fit bearings requires a shop press; forcing it with improvised tools can damage the frame.
- **Wheel truing and building** — small adjustments are learnable, but a wheel that's badly out of true or has a broken spoke is worth a shop's attention.

None of these are permanent no-gos — plenty of home mechanics eventually take them on. They're just not where a beginner should start.

## Building the Habit

The hardest part of bike maintenance isn't the mechanical skill — it's remembering to do it before something fails. Most riders start strong, then let the monthly checks slide once the new-bike enthusiasm wears off.

Two things make the habit stick: a simple log (even a note on your phone with dates) so you're not guessing when you last checked something, and a system that tracks mileage automatically so the reminder comes to you instead of relying on memory.
