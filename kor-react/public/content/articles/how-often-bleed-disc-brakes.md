# How Often to Bleed Disc Brakes (MTB & Road)

Bleed disc brakes every 6–12 months as a baseline — or immediately when the lever feels spongy, mushy, or travels farther than usual before the brakes engage. Unlike chain wear or pad thickness, brake fluid degrades on a calendar schedule, not a mileage one. Time and trail conditions drive the interval more than ride count.

Here's what to know about mineral oil vs. DOT fluid, the symptoms that mean bleed-now, and how to decide whether to DIY or book a shop visit.

![Mountain bike hydraulic disc brake caliper and rotor detail close-up on a dusty trail](/images/articles/how-often-bleed-disc-brakes.webp)

## Baseline Interval: Every 6–12 Months

For most riders, bleeding disc brakes once a year keeps performance consistent. If you're riding year-round, in wet conditions, or logging sustained descents where brakes see repeated heat, a bleed every 6 months is the smarter call.

A few factors push you toward the shorter end of the range:

- **High-volume wet riding**: Water contamination accelerates fluid degradation, especially in DOT systems (more on this below).
- **Multi-day or loaded rides**: Sustained heat from long descents degrades fluid unevenly across the caliper pistons and raises moisture content faster.
- **Racing or performance riding**: When stopping power is safety-critical, a pre-season bleed is worth the hour.
- **Brakes that sat unused**: A bike stored for several months can develop moisture infiltration in the fluid, resulting in a mushy lever the moment you ride it again.

For casual trail riders doing light mileage in dry conditions, once a year — typically before peak riding season — is usually enough.

## Mineral Oil vs. DOT Fluid: Why the Fluid Type Changes Everything

The brake fluid your system uses changes the service schedule significantly. Mixing types destroys seals, so knowing which one you have is step one.

### Mineral Oil (Shimano, Magura)

Mineral oil is hydrophobic — it does not absorb moisture from the atmosphere. That means it stays cleaner longer and is more forgiving of delayed bleed intervals. Shimano recommends fluid changes roughly every 1–2 years, and for average-use trail riding, their mineral-oil systems (XT, XTR, SLX, Deore) often perform well at that cadence.

Mineral oil is also less corrosive to paint and seals, making it lower-risk to handle and easier to top up if you need to do it yourself.

### DOT Fluid (SRAM, Hayes, Clarks, Most Road Systems)

DOT fluid is hygroscopic — it actively absorbs moisture from the air over time. That absorbed moisture lowers the fluid's boiling point, which is exactly why brakes on a long, sustained descent can progressively fade: the fluid has been heating, and degraded DOT fluid boils at a lower temperature than fresh fluid.

SRAM recommends bleeding their DOT systems every **6–12 months** for regular riders, and at minimum annually. DOT 4 and DOT 5.1 are compatible with each other and commonly used; DOT 5 is silicone-based and incompatible with most bike brake seals — don't use it.

DOT fluid is caustic to skin and paint. Handle it carefully and rinse immediately on contact.

### The One Rule

Never mix mineral oil and DOT fluid. They are incompatible and will swell or destroy caliper seals. If you're unsure which your bike uses, check the reservoir cap or the brake manufacturer's spec sheet before adding anything.

## Signs Your Brakes Need Bleeding Right Now

A calendar interval is a baseline, not the only trigger. These symptoms mean bleed regardless of when you last serviced the system:

**Spongy or mushy lever feel** is the clearest sign of air in the hydraulic line. Fluid is incompressible; air is not. Any sponginess means air has entered the system and is absorbing lever force instead of transmitting it to the caliper.

**Increased lever travel** — if the lever now reaches closer to your bar or grip before braking engages — indicates either worn pads, air in the system, or both. Check pad thickness first (see [When to Replace Bike Brake Pads](/articles/when-to-replace-brake-pads)), and if the pads are fine, bleed.

**Slow or partial lever return**: After releasing the brake, the lever should snap back to its resting position quickly. A lever that returns slowly or hangs mid-travel suggests thickening or contaminated fluid, or a sticking caliper piston that needs cleaning alongside the bleed.

**Fading on descents**: Brakes that feel solid at the trailhead but grow progressively spongy during a long descent have fluid that is overheating. This is more common with DOT systems and usually means the fluid is moisture-degraded and needs full replacement, not just a bleed-and-top-off.

**Inconsistent bite point**: Brakes that feel different ride to ride, or that fade and recover unpredictably during a single ride, have fluid that can no longer maintain consistent pressure. A full bleed restores the firm, repeatable bite you expect.

## Why Bleeding Is a Calendar Job, Not a Mileage Job

Chain wear scales with miles ridden. Brake fluid degradation does not. Moisture infiltration into DOT systems happens continuously — whether your bike is on the trail or hanging in the garage. Mineral-oil systems are more stable, but seal degradation and ambient humidity still affect them over months and years.

This is why brake manufacturers specify bleed intervals in months, not miles. It's also why a bike used only on dry summer trails might need a bleed after a humid winter sitting unused — not because of miles, but because of time.

## DIY vs. Shop Bleed: Which Makes Sense

Bleeding disc brakes is an intermediate-level task. It requires the correct fluid, a manufacturer bleed kit, and about 30–45 minutes per wheel. Most LBS mechanics can do both wheels as a walk-in job in under an hour.

**DIY is reasonable if** you're comfortable with the process, have the right bleed kit for your brake brand, and are using a mineral-oil system (lower-risk fluid handling). Shimano and SRAM publish free bleed guides, and the procedure is well-documented on YouTube.

**Defer to a shop if** you're using a DOT system and new to the process, you're preparing for a high-stakes ride or race, or you've bled it yourself and the lever still feels spongy — sometimes a shop bleed with fresh fluid resolves lingering issues that a DIY bleed doesn't fully clear.

## FAQs

### How often should you bleed MTB disc brakes?

Every 6–12 months is the standard baseline for mountain bike riders. SRAM DOT systems need attention at the 6-month mark for active riders; Shimano mineral-oil systems can often stretch to 12–24 months. Bleed sooner if you notice a spongy lever, more travel than usual, or fading on long descents — symptoms trump the schedule.

### What's the difference between mineral oil and DOT fluid intervals?

DOT fluid is hygroscopic (absorbs airborne moisture), which degrades its boiling point and performance over time — bleed every 6–12 months. Mineral oil is hydrophobic (repels moisture), so it stays cleaner longer — annual or biennial bleeds are often enough. Never mix the two; they use incompatible seal materials.

### Can I top off brake fluid instead of doing a full bleed?

Topping off the reservoir replaces volume but doesn't remove air or flush degraded fluid from the caliper and hose. If you have a spongy lever, degraded feel, or fade, a full bleed is needed. Use only the fluid type specified for your system — adding the wrong fluid damages seals.

### How do I know if air is in my brake lines?

A spongy or mushy lever under braking is the primary indicator. Air is compressible; fluid is not. When air is in the line, some of your lever force compresses the air bubble rather than actuating the caliper, reducing braking power and making the lever feel inconsistent. A full bleed removes the air and restores the firm, progressive feel.
