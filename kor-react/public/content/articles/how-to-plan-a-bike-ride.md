# How to Plan a Bike Ride: Route, Gear & Bike Prep

Planning a bike ride comes down to eight steps: pick a distance and elevation that match your fitness, choose a route, check the weather window, build your kit, fuel correctly, run a pre-ride bike check, tell someone your route, and log the ride after. Do all eight and you'll have a ride you can finish strong. Skip any of them and the day gets harder than it needs to be.

![Two cyclists on a scenic mountain road with wide valley landscape and clear blue sky, outdoor natural lighting](/images/articles/how-to-plan-a-bike-ride.webp)

## Step 1: Match Distance and Elevation to Your Fitness

The most common planning mistake is picking a distance without accounting for elevation. A flat 40-mile road ride and a hilly 25-miler with 3,000 feet of climbing can take the same amount of time but demand completely different things from your body.

**Distance baseline:** Your comfortable weekly mileage is the honest starting point. A good target for a solo day ride is 10–20% beyond your recent long ride, not double it. If your longest recent effort was 30 miles, a 35–36 mile ride is the right stretch. Fifty miles is a recipe for bonking.

**Elevation:** A general guide is 100 feet per mile is moderate; 200+ feet per mile is challenging. Look at the elevation *profile* in your route planner, not just the total gain number. Climbs stacked into the back half of a route are far harder to manage than the same gain spread across the whole ride.

**Time estimates:** Build your time estimate before you set a start time. Use 10–12 mph for mixed terrain, 14–16 mph for flat road, 6–10 mph for technical MTB singletrack. Then add 20% for stops, navigation, mechanicals, and the occasional wrong turn. Underestimating time leads to rides that stretch into darkness and families sending search texts.

For rides that push your distance ceiling significantly, see [How to Prepare for a Long Bike Ride](/articles/prepare-for-a-long-bike-ride) for the progressive training and bike prep approach that gets you to the finish.

## Step 2: Choose and Vet Your Route

You don't have to build every route from scratch. Three tools cover most use cases.

**Strava Routes** surfaces popular local rides using heatmap data from millions of logged efforts. Filter by distance, elevation, and surface type. If you already track rides on Strava, this integrates with your history and feeds directly into KOR's component wear tracking — planned mileage automatically becomes maintenance data.

**Komoot** is the strongest tool for off-road planning. It shows surface types, difficulty ratings for each segment, and syncs cleanly with Garmin and Wahoo head units. The freemium tier gives you one free region; for riders who stay in a defined area, it's often enough.

**Ride with GPS** is the best option for complex routes and multi-day planning. Detailed cue sheet support, surface-type granularity, and point-to-point routing with turn-by-turn nav make it the standard for bikepacking and long gravel events. A full side-by-side comparison is in [Best Cycling Route Planners](/articles/cycling-route-planning-apps).

**Before you finalize any route, answer these questions:**
- Where's the nearest bike shop if something fails mid-ride?
- Are there water and food stops, or do you need to carry everything?
- What are the bail-out points if the weather turns or you bonk early?
- What's the surface — road, gravel, hardpack, technical rocky singletrack?
- Does the route cross terrain that requires permits or has seasonal closures?

Download your route with offline maps before you leave. Cell coverage fails exactly where you most need navigation, and cloud-dependent apps are unreliable in remote areas.

## Step 3: Read the Weather Window

Weather rewrites plans. Check the forecast the night before and again the morning of your ride — not just for rain probability, but for wind direction and strength, temperature range across the full ride window, and the timing of any weather changes.

**Wind direction matters more than wind speed.** A 15 mph headwind on a tired return leg turns an enjoyable ride into a grind. Plan to ride into the wind on the way out and take the tailwind on the way back when your legs are spent.

**Temperature range:** Dress for the coldest moment of the ride — usually the first 30 minutes before you warm up, and any long descents where you stop generating heat. A packable windproof vest or shell covers most weather contingencies for road riding without much weight or bulk.

**Rain:** Wet roads cut braking distances by 30–50%. Increase following distance, reduce cornering speed, and be especially cautious on metal surfaces — manhole covers, painted lines, and rail crossings become genuinely slippery when wet. The maintenance angle: wet riding accelerates chain wear two to three times faster than dry conditions. If you ride in the rain regularly, your service intervals arrive sooner. [Cycling in the Rain](/articles/cycling-in-the-rain) covers both the riding technique and the post-rain care that protects your drivetrain.

## Step 4: Calculate Your Fuel and Hydration

Bonking — hitting glycogen depletion — is the most common reason rides fall apart after the route planning is done. Your body stores roughly 60–90 minutes of glycogen at moderate aerobic effort. Everything after that depends on what you're carrying and consuming.

**Carbohydrate target:** For rides over 90 minutes, plan for 60–90 grams of carbohydrates per hour. That's roughly two standard gels, one gel plus a banana, or a combination of bars, chews, and real food. Your gut needs to practice absorbing carbs while exercising — if you haven't trained with nutrition, start at the lower end and work up gradually.

**Hydration:** Drink 16–24 oz (500–750 ml) per hour in mild weather. In heat or humidity, that rises to 24–32 oz. Electrolytes become important beyond 90 minutes — late-ride cramping is almost always a sodium deficit, not just dehydration, and plain water won't fix it.

**Pre-ride fuel:** Eat a carbohydrate-heavy meal 2–3 hours before your start time. A small top-up 30–60 minutes before — a banana, a gel, a small bowl of oatmeal — keeps glycogen levels high without causing GI distress during the first hard effort of the day.

**Always carry a buffer.** Pack 20% more food than your math suggests. Longer-than-expected rides, effort spikes on climbs, and cold weather all burn through calories faster than any formula predicts.

## Step 5: Pack the Right Kit

What you carry scales with ride length, terrain, and how far from civilization you are.

### The Non-Negotiable Minimum

These go on every ride, no exceptions:

- 2 tire levers
- 1 spare tube (correct size and valve type for your wheels)
- Mini pump or CO2 inflator with at least one extra cartridge
- Multitool with Allen keys (2/2.5/3/4/5/6 mm), chain breaker, flathead
- 1 quick link matching your chain speed (9s, 10s, 11s, or 12s — carry the right one)
- Phone with route downloaded offline
- ID and emergency cash or card
- Water for the full ride or a confirmed refill point

### For Rides Over 2 Hours

- Second spare tube
- Patch kit (belt and suspenders for remote rides)
- Packable jacket or windproof vest
- Extra food with 20% buffer beyond your fueling plan
- Electrolyte tabs or mix

### For MTB or Remote Routes

- Tubeless plug kit (plugs, small syringe, valve core tool)
- Emergency whistle
- Basic first aid (athletic tape, blister pads, alcohol wipes)
- Offline maps covering not just the route but surrounding area for navigation if you get off track

Full packing lists broken down by ride length and discipline are in [What to Bring on a Bike Ride](/articles/what-to-bring-on-a-bike-ride).

## Step 6: Run a Pre-Ride Bike Check

A mechanical failure mid-ride is more than inconvenient. On a remote trail, a snapped chain 12 miles in with no quick link in your bag becomes a long hike. On a group ride, it makes you the rider who holds everyone up.

**The M-check** is the standard pre-ride walkthrough. Start at the front axle, work upward through the bars and headset, across the drivetrain and bottom bracket area, then back to the saddle/post and rear axle and brakes. The full step-by-step — including what each finding means and what to do about it — is in [The 5-Minute Pre-Ride Bike Check](/articles/pre-ride-bike-check).

**The M-check is not a substitute for maintenance.** The pre-ride check tells you if something is immediately wrong: a cracked tire, a loose axle, a brake that won't engage. It won't tell you that your chain is approaching the 0.5% wear threshold and is already eating your cassette, or that your disc pads are at their 1.5 mm limit and have one hard stop left in them.

**A maintenance-ready bike is step zero for ride planning.** The [Bike Maintenance Schedule](/articles/bike-maintenance-schedule) covers every component interval, from chain replacement to fork service. No guessing, no spreadsheet.

## Step 7: Tell Someone Your Route

This takes 30 seconds and matters most on solo rides in remote areas. Share your route — or at minimum your start point, planned end point, and expected return time — with someone who will follow up if they don't hear from you.

For rides that stay in cell coverage, a Strava LiveTrack session or Garmin LiveTrack link shares your live GPS position with anyone you send it to. Enable it before you roll out.

For remote trails or bikepacking routes outside cell coverage, a satellite communicator (Garmin inReach Mini, SPOT Gen4) adds a meaningful safety margin. The cost is low compared to the alternative. For a full introduction to bikepacking logistics, including emergency communication, see [Bikepacking for Beginners](/articles/bikepacking-for-beginners).

## Step 8: Log the Ride and Build Your Dataset

The post-ride log is where planning gets smarter over time. Logging to Strava, Garmin Connect, or your platform of choice builds a personal dataset: actual pace vs. estimated, nutrition that worked or didn't, segments that were harder than expected.

For KOR users, every logged ride automatically updates component wear — your chain mileage, brake hours, and tire mileage stay current without any manual entry. Your next pre-ride check reflects real wear, not a rough guess.

**Review questions after every long effort:**
- Did your pace estimate match reality?
- Did you bonk, or did you finish with food left over?
- Did any mechanical issues surface mid-ride?
- What would you pack differently?
- Did the route difficulty match your expectation going in?

One honest post-ride assessment improves five future rides.

## Checklists You Can Copy

### The Night Before

- [ ] Route confirmed — distance, elevation, surface type, bail-out points
- [ ] Weather checked for full ride window including wind direction
- [ ] Fueling plan calculated (60–90 g carbs/hr for rides over 90 min)
- [ ] Kit bag packed and checked against the minimum list
- [ ] Route loaded and downloaded offline on device or phone

### Morning of the Ride

- [ ] Weather rechecked (conditions change overnight)
- [ ] Pre-ride meal eaten 2–3 hours before start
- [ ] Bike M-check complete
- [ ] Tire pressure confirmed for the surface type and discipline
- [ ] Route shared with someone who will check in if you're not back

### Post-Ride

- [ ] Ride logged to your platform of choice
- [ ] Bike wiped down, especially after rain or mud
- [ ] Consumed supplies restocked (tube, CO2 cartridge, food)
- [ ] Notes made on what to do differently next time

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I pick the right distance for my fitness level?**
Start from your recent long ride and add 10–20%, not double it. If your current comfortable long ride is 25 miles, plan for 28–30 miles. Consistent progressive increases get you to longer distances safely. Jumping straight to 50 miles when your current ceiling is 25 is how rides end in a car pickup call.

**What's the single most important thing to pack on a bike ride?**
A spare tube and something to inflate it with. Every other repair is optional or improvised. A flat you can't fix ends the ride immediately. After that, a quick link matching your chain speed — a snapped chain without a quick link is also a walk home.

**How do I plan a bike ride without a GPS head unit?**
Download your route on Komoot or Ride with GPS, cache the offline map on your phone, and mount your phone to your bars with an inexpensive handlebar mount. Most apps support offline navigation on the free tier. A $12 mount and a free app do the navigation job most casual riders need.

**How far in advance should I check the weather?**
Check 24–48 hours out for general planning — will rain be a factor, what's the temperature range? Then check again the morning of for wind direction and exact timing of any weather changes. Weather apps become reliably accurate inside the 24-hour window. Planning around a 5-day forecast that shows possible rain is premature.

**When should I do a full bike check before a long ride?**
Run the M-check the morning of. For remote or especially long rides, also check your bike 2–3 days before so you have time to fix anything you find — discovering a frayed brake cable or a cracked tire on the morning of a 70-mile day is a problem with no good solution at 6 a.m.
