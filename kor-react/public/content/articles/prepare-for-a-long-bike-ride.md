# How to Prepare for a Long Bike Ride (Training to Tune-Up)

Preparing for a long bike ride comes down to three things done in advance: building your body to handle the distance, fueling correctly on the day, and getting your bike ready before you show up at the start. Skip any one of them and the ride gets hard in a way that has nothing to do with fitness.

## Build Distance the Right Way

The 10% rule is the most practical training guideline for cyclists who want to go longer without getting hurt: increase your weekly distance or your longest single ride by no more than 10% per week.

If your current long ride is 40 miles, next week's long effort caps at 44. The week after, 48. It feels slow, but connective tissue — tendons, ligaments, the soft structures around your knees and hips — adapts far slower than your cardiovascular system. Your legs feel fine long before your body is actually ready. Pushing past 10% per week is where overuse injuries come from.

**Practical build schedule:**

- 6 weeks out: establish your baseline long ride
- 4 weeks out: hit your target distance for the first time at easy effort
- 3 weeks out: repeat the distance; note where you struggled
- 2 weeks out: begin bike prep (see below)
- 1 week out: reduce volume by 30–40%; keep intensity moderate
- Ride week: short spin two days before; rest the day before

One long ride per week is enough. The rest of your riding — shorter efforts, recovery spins — builds the aerobic base that makes long days possible.

## Fueling Before the Ride

What you eat in the 24 hours before a long ride matters as much as what you carry. Your muscles store glycogen — carbohydrate fuel — and you want those stores topped off when you start.

The night before a long ride, eat a normal meal with a larger carbohydrate component than usual: pasta, rice, potatoes, bread. You don't need to eat twice your normal amount — just shift the ratio toward carbs and avoid anything that might upset your stomach the next morning (heavy fats, unfamiliar foods, excessive fiber).

The morning of the ride, eat 2–3 hours before you start. A standard pre-ride meal is 600–900 calories, carbohydrate-focused: oatmeal with banana, toast with peanut butter and honey, or rice and eggs. Give your body time to digest before you put it under load.

If your start time doesn't allow a full meal, a smaller snack 30–60 minutes out — a banana, a bar, a gel with water — is better than starting on empty.

---

## Fueling During the Ride

Your body can only store roughly 90 minutes of glycogen at moderate riding intensity. After that, you need to replace carbohydrates continuously or your power drops and your mood goes with it. This is called bonking, and it's completely avoidable.

**The fueling math:**

- Start eating at 45–60 minutes; don't wait until you're hungry
- Target 60 g of carbohydrate per hour during sustained efforts
- For rides over 3 hours at race or event pace, move toward 90 g/hr — a mix of glucose and fructose sources handles this better than gels alone
- Real food (rice cakes, banana, PB&J, boiled potatoes with salt) sits better on 4+ hour rides than gel overload

**Sources of 60 g carbohydrate:**
- 1 large banana + 1 gel ≈ 57 g
- 2 standard gels ≈ 50–54 g
- 1 energy bar ≈ 40–45 g (brand-dependent)
- 500 mL sports drink ≈ 35–40 g

Combine sources to hit your target. Carry more than you think you'll need — dropping into a calorie hole in the final third of a ride is how long days get miserable.

## Hydration

Drink before you're thirsty. Thirst lags behind actual dehydration by 1–2%, and at 2% dehydration, power output measurably drops.

**Per-hour targets:**
- Easy effort, mild day: 16–20 oz
- Moderate effort or warm: 20–24 oz
- Hard effort or hot: 24–32 oz

Beyond 3 hours, plain water isn't enough. Add electrolytes — sodium, potassium, magnesium — via tabs, powder, or a sports drink. The salt maintains fluid balance and prevents hyponatremia (a rare but real risk when you drink large volumes of plain water over many hours).

Pre-ride hydration: drink 16 oz with your pre-ride meal, and another 8–16 oz in the final 30 minutes before you roll.

## Bike Prep: Two Weeks Out

This is the part most riders skip, and it's the reason so many long rides end early. Two weeks out is the right window — early enough to catch anything that needs a shop visit, late enough that wear since your last check matters.

**Check these items:**

**Chain wear.** A chain at or past 0.5% stretch is a problem on a long ride. Under load, a worn chain skips on worn cassette teeth and can drop or snap at the worst moment. Measure it with a chain checker. If it's at the limit, replace it before the ride — not after. See the full [bike maintenance schedule](/articles/bike-maintenance-schedule) for what intervals look like by discipline.

**Brake pads.** Road brake pads below 1 mm of compound and mountain disc pads below 1.5 mm are on borrowed time. Long descents generate more heat than short ones, and thin pads fade faster. A 4-hour ride with 4,000 feet of descent is not the ride to find out your pads are done. Check them with a flashlight or pull the wheel.

**Tires.** Look for cracking sidewalls, embedded glass or thorns, and tread depth. A tire that's fine for a 20-mile training loop may not be the right call for a 100-miler. If you're running tubeless, check sealant level — it evaporates over months and leaves you with no protection. See [when to replace bike tires](/articles/when-to-replace-bike-tires) for specific wear indicators.

**Cables and housing.** Sluggish shifting or brake pull that requires more lever force than usual points to frayed cables or contaminated housing. Both get worse, not better, over a long effort.

**Bolts.** Saddle, seatpost clamp, stem, bars. Check that nothing is loose. Torque to spec if you have a torque wrench — carbon components have specific limits.

If anything needs a shop, go two weeks out. Shops get booked; a week-before scramble usually ends with "we can't take it until after your event."

Run a full [pre-ride bike check](/articles/pre-ride-bike-check) the day before the ride — it takes five minutes and catches anything missed in the shop prep.

## Pacing

Going too hard in the first hour is the most common mistake on long rides. You feel good, conditions are good, and you've been anticipating this for weeks. The temptation is real.

A useful rule: your first hour should feel easier than your goal pace. If you're riding by perceived effort, the first half should feel like a 6 out of 10. If you're using a power meter, stay in zones 2–3 until the midpoint, then spend your matches in the final third if you have them.

**Signs you're going too hard early:**
- Breathing harder than you can hold a conversation
- Burning sensation in legs before mile 15
- Eating and drinking feels like extra effort

On charity rides and cycling events, it's easy to get swept up in groups moving faster than your goal pace. Let them go. You'll pass many of them later.

For fully unsupported rides, plan your effort around your turnaround point. The ride back always feels longer.

## Contingency Planning

Long rides expose you to more variables than short ones. Plan for problems.

**Know the route.** Download it to your GPS device before you leave. Cell coverage disappears in the places you're most likely to be lost. Know the general direction of your turn-around point and the location of any bailout options.

**Share your plan.** Tell someone where you're going, your route, and when to expect you back. Leave a contact number for your pick-up spot if one exists.

**Money and ID.** Always. Even if you've packed every contingency, unexpected mechanicals, medical emergencies, and miscalculations happen. A card and $40 cash gets you out of most situations.

**Know what you'll do if your bike breaks.** For a frame failure or unrepairable mechanical, you need a plan: who to call, whether you have a rideshare app loaded, whether your route has a town with a shop. See [what to bring on a bike ride](/articles/what-to-bring-on-a-bike-ride) for the full repair kit list.

**Weather window.** Check the forecast the night before and the morning of. On long rides, afternoon thunderstorms in mountain terrain are a real safety issue. Know your turnaround time relative to typical storm build.
