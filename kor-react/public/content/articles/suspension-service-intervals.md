# Fork & Shock Service Intervals: The Hour-Based Guide

Your fork and rear shock are the only components on your bike serviced by hours, not miles. Lower-leg and air-can service runs roughly every 50 hours of riding; a full damper rebuild is due every 100–200 hours. Miss those windows and you're not just riding a slower bike — you're riding toward stanchion damage and a repair bill that dwarfs the cost of routine upkeep.

![Mountain bike fork suspension detail close-up on a trail](/images/articles/suspension-service-intervals.webp)

## Why Suspension Is Serviced in Hours, Not Miles

A drivetrain component like a chain wears proportionally to distance traveled — more miles means more metal-on-metal contact cycles. Suspension is different. The oil inside your fork and shock degrades based on how many compression and rebound cycles it absorbs, and those cycles stack up regardless of your speed. A brutal ten-mile descent with 1,000 small-bump impacts degrades the damping oil more than a flat fifty-mile cruise.

That's why Fox, RockShox, and every major suspension manufacturer lists service intervals in ride hours. Hours give a meaningful unit of measure where mileage doesn't.

## Lower-Leg Service and Air-Can Clean: ~50 Hours

The first and most frequent service for your fork is the lower-leg service. For the rear shock, it's the air-can strip and clean. Both are due approximately every 50 ride hours.

**What this service includes:**
- Remove the fork lowers (lower legs)
- Drain and replace the bath oil — the oil volume that lubricates the stanchions
- Clean the foam rings and seals, replace if worn
- Inspect stanchion surfaces for scratches or scoring
- For the rear shock: remove the air can, clean the negative and positive air chambers, replace the air-can seal

This is a service many experienced riders handle themselves with the right oil and a set of seal tools. It takes 30–60 minutes for a fork lower-leg service and roughly the same for a rear shock air-can clean. Fox and RockShox both publish service manuals with exact oil volumes and viscosities for each model year and product line.

If you're riding 6–8 hours per week, that means a lower-leg service roughly every 6–8 weeks. If you ride 2–3 hours per week, you might go 4–6 months between services — but don't skip them.

## Full Damper Service: 100–200 Hours

The full rebuild — also called a full service or damper service — is a deeper job due every 100–200 hours depending on brand, model, and how demanding your riding is.

**What this service includes:**
- Complete disassembly of the damper cartridge
- Replace all internal seals, O-rings, and bushings
- Clean and inspect all internal components
- Refill with fresh damping oil at factory-specified viscosity and volume
- Full air-spring rebuild on the rear shock

Fox recommends full damper service at 125 hours for the Float and Grip2 series under normal riding conditions. RockShox calls for inspection at 100 hours and full service at 200 hours for Charger and Thuderbuss dampers, though wet or aggressive conditions move that interval earlier. For rear shocks, RockShox recommends a full service at 200 hours; Fox targets 125 hours for most of their shock lineup.

Full damper service typically requires a shop — the tools, press jigs, and cleanliness required are beyond most home setups. Budget for one shop service per season if you're riding regularly.

## Symptoms of Overdue Suspension Service

You don't always need to count hours precisely. Your fork and shock will tell you when they're overdue. Know these warning signs:

**Stiction** — Instead of moving smoothly over small bumps, the fork feels sticky or hesitant, then suddenly releases. That grabbing feel at the top of the stroke is a sign of contaminated or degraded bath oil and worn foam rings.

**Loss of small-bump sensitivity** — The fork or shock that once tracked small rocks and roots now feels harsh or unresponsive over the same terrain. Degraded damping oil loses its ability to control fast, low-force movements.

**Oil weep** — A faint film of oil on the stanchions that builds up faster than normal, or small droplets at the seal line, means seals are no longer holding cleanly. This is both a contamination sign and a warning that the bath oil level is dropping.

**Visible contamination** — Mud and grit packed into the seal area, or dark greasy buildup at the stanchion-lower interface, mean contaminants are already working past the wipers.

**Audible changes** — A clunking, sucking, or gurgling sound from the fork mid-travel suggests air is mixing with oil inside the damper — a sign the damper seal is compromised.

Any one of these symptoms means a service is overdue. Multiple symptoms together mean do it now.

## Fox vs. RockShox: Quick Reference Intervals

| Brand | Service Level | Interval |
|-------|--------------|----------|
| Fox | Lower-leg service | 50 hours |
| Fox | Full damper rebuild | 125 hours |
| RockShox | Lower-leg service | 50 hours |
| RockShox | Full damper rebuild | 100–200 hours |

Wet and muddy riding accelerates every interval above. If a significant portion of your riding is in wet conditions, on bike park brake bumps, or on gnarly technical terrain, cut these intervals by 20–30%.

## Keeping Tuning Notes

One of the most underrated aspects of suspension maintenance is recording your current setup before a service. Fork pressure, rebound clicks, compression settings, sag percentage — these can take hours to re-dial if you lose them during a rebuild.

Log your setup alongside your service history so you can hand exact numbers to your mechanic or reference them when you do the work yourself. A note that says "lower-leg service at 48 hours, rebound 6 clicks out, pressure 78 psi" is worth real money the next time you're chasing a feel you lost.

## The Cost of Neglecting Suspension Service

A lower-leg service costs $30–60 in materials to DIY and $60–120 at a shop. A full damper service runs $80–200 at a shop depending on fork model.

A new stanchion? That's $150–400 depending on the fork. A replacement damper cartridge? $150–300. New bushings — which wear quickly when oil is contaminated — can run $40–100 per set.

More significantly, riding on contaminated or dry oil accelerates bushing wear, stanchion scratching, and seal damage simultaneously. Each hour of riding on a neglected fork multiplies the cost of the next service. A $70 lower-leg service skipped at 50 hours often becomes a $400 stanchion replacement by 100 hours.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I service my MTB suspension fork?

Lower-leg service (bath oil change and seal clean) is due approximately every 50 ride hours. Full damper service is due every 100–200 hours depending on manufacturer and riding conditions. For most riders doing 6–8 hours per week, that means a lower-leg service every 6–8 weeks and a full rebuild roughly once per year.

### What happens if I don't service my suspension?

The bath oil becomes contaminated, foam rings dry out, and grit works past the seals onto the stanchions. The result is stiction, lost small-bump sensitivity, and eventual stanchion scratching. A $70 lower-leg service left undone can lead to a $300–400 stanchion replacement within a season.

### Can I do fork service myself?

Lower-leg service is a common DIY job that requires the correct bath oil (typically 5–10 weight), seal tools, and a clean workspace. Full damper service is more involved — most riders use a shop for the damper rebuild because it requires precision tooling and contamination-free oil filling.

### Do Fox and RockShox have different service intervals?

Their intervals are similar: both recommend lower-leg service around 50 hours and full damper service between 100–200 hours. Fox publishes 125 hours for the Grip2 damper; RockShox targets 100–200 hours for Charger dampers. Wet or aggressive conditions make earlier service prudent for both brands.

### Does my rear shock need the same service schedule as my fork?

Yes. Rear shocks follow a similar interval: air-can clean at ~50 hours, full damper service at 100–200 hours. Signs of a neglected shock include a clunk at the top of the stroke, inconsistent sag measurement, and visible oil around the air sleeve.
