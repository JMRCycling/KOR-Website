# What to Bring on a Bike Ride: Packing Lists by Distance

What you need on a bike ride depends almost entirely on how long you'll be out. A quick 45-minute spin needs a phone and a key. An all-day adventure needs nutrition, layers, repair parts, money, and a plan. The lists below are tiered by ride length so you can pack exactly what you need — and nothing you don't.

![Cycling gear flat-lay on a surface: pump, multitool, tube, energy bars, water bottle, and phone, natural lighting](/images/articles/what-to-bring-on-a-bike-ride.webp)

---

## Packing List: Under 1 Hour

Short rides close to home have minimal requirements. You're never far from help, and the consequences of forgetting something are low.

**Essentials:**
- [ ] Charged phone (in pocket or jersey)
- [ ] House or car key
- [ ] Water bottle (16–24 oz is enough)
- [ ] ID and a few dollars (or card)
- [ ] Helmet and gloves

**Optional but smart:**
- [ ] A CO2 inflator and one spare tube — even on a 30-minute ride, a flat on the wrong block is a long walk

Skip the repair kit if you're riding loops close to your door. But the moment you venture into unfamiliar territory or farther than a 20-minute walk from home, the repair kit becomes non-negotiable.

---

## Packing List: 1–3 Hours

This is the most common ride length, and it's the window where getting caught under-packed becomes a real problem. You're far enough out that a mechanical or a bonk turns a good ride into a bad day.

**Always bring:**
- [ ] 2 water bottles (or 1 bottle + hydration bladder for MTB)
- [ ] Nutrition: 1 energy bar or gel per hour (see food math below)
- [ ] Spare tube (road) or plug kit + tube (tubeless MTB)
- [ ] Tire levers (2)
- [ ] CO2 inflator or hand pump
- [ ] Multitool with chain breaker
- [ ] Quick link (matching your chain speed)
- [ ] Phone (charged)
- [ ] ID, cash or card, insurance card
- [ ] Sunscreen applied before you leave; small tube if the ride is in full sun

**Add if conditions call for it:**
- [ ] Arm warmers or a packable rain shell (weather can shift fast on rides over 2 hours)
- [ ] Extra food if the route passes no stores or cafes
- [ ] Lights if your return time is uncertain

A jersey with three rear pockets holds most of this. For MTB, a small hip pack or hydration pack carries the repair kit without cluttering your bike.

---

## Packing List: All Day (4+ Hours)

Full-day rides require real planning. The gear list grows, the food math becomes critical, and you need contingencies for weather, mechanicals, and energy swings.

**Core kit:**
- [ ] 2–3 water bottles or a 2L hydration pack
- [ ] Nutrition for the full distance (see food math below)
- [ ] Cash and card (you'll likely stop somewhere)
- [ ] Fully charged phone
- [ ] ID and emergency contact info

**Repair kit:**
- [ ] 2 spare tubes
- [ ] Tire levers
- [ ] Mini pump (CO2 runs out; a pump doesn't)
- [ ] Multitool with chain breaker
- [ ] 2 quick links
- [ ] Patch kit as backup
- [ ] Tire boot (foil wrapper works; a real boot is better)
- [ ] Spare derailleur hanger (matches your bike)

**Comfort and safety:**
- [ ] Packable rain shell
- [ ] Arm or knee warmers
- [ ] Sunscreen (reapply every 2 hours in direct sun)
- [ ] Front and rear lights even if you plan a daytime return
- [ ] Small first-aid kit (bandages, antiseptic wipe, elastic bandage)

For long-distance and bikepacking rides, see the more detailed advice in [How to Prepare for a Long Bike Ride](/articles/prepare-for-a-long-bike-ride).

## The Repair Kit Minimum

No matter the ride length, the five-item repair kit covers the vast majority of trailside mechanicals. Carry all five or carry none — a tube without levers is useless; a pump without a tube saves nothing.

**The five essentials:**

1. **Spare tube** — matched to your tire size and valve type (Presta or Schrader). Road riders: one tube per tire on longer rides. MTB: one tube per ride plus a plug kit if running tubeless.
2. **Tire levers** — two plastic levers. Cheap and light; don't skip them.
3. **Pump or CO2** — CO2 inflates fast (good for racing or cold days), but you only get one shot per cartridge. A mini hand pump is slower but reusable. Carry both on all-day rides.
4. **Multitool with chain breaker** — a standard 9-in-1 covers saddle height, stem bolts, derailleur cable adjustments, and chain breaks. Make sure yours has a chain breaker built in.
5. **Quick link** — if your chain snaps and you don't have a quick link, you're not riding home. Match the speed (e.g., 11-speed SRAM chain needs an 11-speed quick link). Costs a dollar; lives in your kit forever.

A spoke wrench and zip ties take up almost no space and solve problems nothing else can. Consider them permanent kit residents on longer rides.

## Food and Water Math

Bonking (running out of glycogen mid-ride) is entirely avoidable if you do the math before you leave.

**Water:**
- Moderate effort, mild weather: about 16–20 oz per hour
- Hard effort or hot day: 24–28 oz per hour
- Above 3 hours: consider electrolyte tabs or a mix, not just plain water

A standard 24 oz bottle lasts about 1 hour at moderate effort. Plan your refill stops accordingly. On routes with no water sources, carry extra or cache a bottle in your car at a midpoint.

**Food:**
- Under 1 hour: nothing required if you've eaten in the last 2–3 hours
- 1–2 hours: one bar or gel is enough to keep power steady
- 2–3 hours: target 60 g of carbohydrate per hour from the second hour onward
- 3+ hours: 60–90 g of carbs per hour; real food (rice cakes, banana, PB&J) handles this better than gel overload

The practical shorthand: one gel or bar per hour of riding past the 45-minute mark. If you're stopping at a cafe or store, plan around that stop — but don't rely on it as your only source.

Front-load your nutrition. Eating at mile 15 of a 40-mile ride is too late. Eat before you're hungry, and drink before you're thirsty.

## Phone, ID, and Money

These three items belong in your kit regardless of ride length.

**Phone:** Keep it charged before you leave. 20% battery is not enough for a 3-hour ride with navigation running. If you're using your phone for Strava and maps simultaneously, it drains fast. A small USB-C battery bank (10,000 mAh, 6 oz) solves this on all-day rides.

**ID:** If you crash, first responders need to know who you are. A medical ID bracelet or a physical card in your kit is a good habit. Put an emergency contact in your phone as "ICE [Name]."

**Money:** Cash beats card in rural areas, gas stations, and older cafes. Carry at least $20. A card is faster elsewhere. Store them in a small zip-lock or a mini wallet — jersey pockets fill with sweat, and loose cards delaminate.

## What Changes by Discipline

**Road bike:** Weight is the priority. A saddle bag or two jersey pockets hold the core kit. Use CO2 for speed; keep the bag minimal. Clip-on aerobars and carbon wheels require specific tube valve extenders — check before you pack.

**Mountain bike:** You're farther from help and the terrain is harder on components. A hip pack or small hydration pack is standard. Add tubeless plugs (Dynaplug or similar), a dropper post pump if applicable, and more food — technical riding burns more than road riding at the same speed.

**Commuter:** You likely don't need a repair kit for a familiar daily route, but carry a lock, lights (always), and cash for emergencies. If you commute in work clothes, a rack and panniers beat a backpack for comfort.

## Before You Roll

Packing the right kit starts with a [pre-ride bike check](/articles/pre-ride-bike-check). A flat tire mid-ride is bad; a worn-out tube that fails in your kit because you haven't checked it is worse. Rotate your spare tubes every season — rubber degrades in a seat bag over time.
