# Dropper Post Maintenance: Service Intervals & Care

Your dropper post needs service every 100–200 ride hours — similar to your fork, but often forgotten because it doesn't rattle or feel harsh until the service interval is well past. Neglect it long enough and you're dealing with saddle sag, sluggish return travel, and side play that makes technical descents feel unstable.

![Mountain biker descending a technical trail with dropper post visible, natural outdoor lighting, action shot](/images/articles/dropper-post-maintenance.webp)

## How Often to Service a Dropper Post

The standard dropper post service interval is 100–200 ride hours depending on your brand, model, and riding conditions. RockShox Reverb models target 100 hours for basic service and 200 hours for a full rebuild. Fox Transfer posts follow a similar schedule. Cable-actuated designs from brands like OneUp and KS tend to be less demanding — many riders extend those to 150–200 hours before performance noticeably drops.

For someone riding 6–8 hours per week, 100 hours means a service roughly every three to four months. At two or three rides per week in the 2-hour range, that same 100 hours stretches to five or six months.

Wet, muddy, or sandy riding moves you toward the shorter end of that range. A summer of dry trail riding on hard pack may let you push closer to 200 hours before you notice anything. Your post will make it clear — see the symptoms section below.

## Daily Care: The Habits That Double Your Service Interval

Small habits after every ride are the single biggest factor in whether your dropper hits 100 hours or 200 hours before needing work.

**Wipe the stanchion after every ride.** The stanchion — the tube that slides in and out of the post body — collects grit and trail debris every time you cycle the post. That contamination gets dragged past the wiper seal on the next actuation. A dry rag wipe after every muddy or dusty ride takes under a minute and meaningfully slows seal wear.

**Store the post at full extension.** Leaving a dropper in the dropped position for extended periods — overnight, through a bike storage season — can let oil slowly migrate past the internal seal into the cable routing or actuator body. Keep the post extended when you're not riding.

**Skip the pressure washer at the clamp junction.** High-pressure water forced into the post-to-frame interface pushes water past the wiper seal into the post body. A gentle rinse or damp cloth around the clamp area is enough to clean it without forcing contamination inside.

These habits won't eliminate service — the oil still breaks down over time — but they will reliably push you from 100-hour services toward 150–200-hour intervals.

## Symptoms That Signal an Overdue Service

Your post tells you when service is due. The symptoms appear gradually, then compound.

**Saddle sag** — You set your saddle height, drop the post, then return it to full extension and the saddle sits 5–15 mm lower than where you set it. This happens because worn internal seals can no longer hold oil pressure behind the stanchion under load. Sag is the most common sign of a tired dropper and usually appears before anything else.

**Slow return** — A healthy post snaps back to full height almost immediately when you release the lever under no load. A post due for service returns sluggishly, especially in cold temperatures. Slow return usually means degraded oil viscosity or a partially blocked return circuit.

**Side play at the saddle** — Grab the saddle and push it side to side. A millimeter or two of play is normal at the saddle rails. Noticeably more than that — especially play you can feel at the post collar — means the internal bushings are worn. Side play accelerates seal wear and gets worse quickly once it appears.

**Lever feel changes** — A lever that suddenly needs more force to actuate, or feels sticky at the top of its travel, is often contaminated cable housing (on cable posts) or a sticking internal valve. On hydraulic posts, a spongy or inconsistent lever usually means air has entered the hydraulic line.

One of these symptoms means service is approaching. Two or more together means schedule it now — continued riding accelerates wear on everything downstream.

## Cable vs. Hydraulic Actuation: What Changes for Service

Most dropper posts use one of two actuation systems, and they have different service priorities.

**Cable-actuated posts** use a mechanical cable to trigger an internal valve that controls a hydraulic or spring cartridge. The cable and housing are the first things to inspect. Cable stretch and housing compression change lever feel before the post internals fail. On a cable-actuated post, checking cable tension and lubing or replacing the housing is part of every service. Contaminated housing can cause sticky or incomplete actuation even when the post itself is fine. These are generally simpler to service at home.

**Hydraulic-actuated posts** — Fox Transfer, RockShox Reverb AXS, and similar — run a sealed hydraulic line from the remote lever to the post. They eliminate cable maintenance entirely but need a bleed if air enters the system. Signs of air in the line include a spongy lever, inconsistent drop height, or the post not returning to full extension on release. Bleeding a hydraulic dropper is similar to bleeding disc brakes: manageable at home if you're comfortable with that work, faster at a shop if you're not.

## What a Full Service Involves

A dropper post service typically includes:

- Remove the post from the frame
- Separate the post body from the stanchion tube
- Drain and replace the internal oil — usually 5–15 ml of medium-weight fork oil (5–15 wt depending on model)
- Inspect and replace internal seals, O-rings, and bushings as needed
- Clean and inspect the internal valve and IFP (internal floating piston) on hydraulic posts
- Reinstall with fresh grease at the wiper seal interface
- Re-bleed the actuator circuit on hydraulic posts
- Reinstall in the frame, re-set cable tension or hydraulic pressure, verify full actuation through range of travel

RockShox publishes detailed service guides for the Reverb, Reverb Stealth, and Reverb AXS. Fox publishes equivalent guides for the Transfer lineup. These PDFs are available on their websites and cover exact oil volumes, torque specs, and tool requirements for each model year.

DIY dropper service is well within reach for a mechanically comfortable rider, especially on popular cable-actuated posts. The main requirements are a clean workspace, the correct oil, and patience during reassembly. A shop is faster if you're unfamiliar with the process, and necessary for posts with difficult frame integration.

## When to Use a Shop

Take your dropper to a shop if:

- The post uses internal cable routing that requires partial frame disassembly to remove
- The lever is hydraulic and you're not comfortable bleeding the system
- The internal valve or IFP has failed — this often requires a factory service kit or warranty claim
- You've completed a DIY service and symptoms persist unchanged

A basic dropper service at a shop runs $40–80 in most markets. A full rebuild including seal replacement and IFP inspection runs $80–150. A replacement post costs $200–500. Service is the right first call in almost every case.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I service my dropper post?

Service your dropper post every 100–200 ride hours depending on brand and conditions. RockShox recommends 100 hours for basic Reverb service; Fox targets a similar interval for the Transfer. For most riders, that means every 3–6 months depending on ride frequency.

### What causes a dropper post to sag?

Sag — where the saddle sits lower than your set height after returning from the dropped position — is almost always worn internal seals that can no longer hold oil pressure. A full service with fresh oil and new seals restores the post to full extension.

### Should I store my dropper post up or down?

Store it at full extension (post up). Leaving the post in the dropped position for extended periods allows oil to migrate past the internal seal over time, which accelerates wear and can contaminate cable routing.

### Can I service a dropper post myself?

Yes, for most posts. Popular models like the RockShox Reverb and OneUp V2 have detailed DIY service guides available from the manufacturer. You need the correct oil weight (usually 5–15 ml of 5–15 wt fork oil), a seal pick, and a clean workspace. Hydraulic actuation posts require a bleed kit if you're addressing air in the system.

### Why is my dropper lever spongy?

A spongy lever on a hydraulic dropper usually means air has entered the hydraulic line. Bleeding the system — the same process as bleeding disc brakes — fixes it. On a cable-actuated post, a spongy or heavy lever typically points to contaminated or compressed cable housing; replace the housing and re-set cable tension.
